# Business Rules and Known Limitations

What the contracts enforce, and what this proof of concept deliberately does not do.

## Business rules

- Only a regulator may register, update or suspend stakeholders.
- Stakeholders are linked to their authenticated Fabric certificate IDs.
- Supported roles are `REGULATOR`, `FARM`, `PROCESSOR`, `LOGISTICS`, `RETAILER` and `ORACLE`.
- Only a `FARM` may create a milk batch, because that is where milk enters the chain and the
  origin recorded at creation is never changed afterwards.
- Batch lifecycle transitions must be validated on-chain.
- Invalid or out-of-order events must be rejected.
- Only `ORACLE` may submit temperature evidence, and the regulator's own peer must endorse it.
- Raw temperature readings must remain off-chain in PostgreSQL, in the database belonging to the
  company that collected them.
- The oracle must canonicalise readings, calculate statistics and compute a SHA-256 hash.
- Only the hash, off-chain reference, statistics and compliance outcome are stored on-chain.
- The contract decides the compliance outcome, not the oracle. The oracle's own reading of the
  numbers stays in its database, and the regulator archives what the contract decided from the
  ledger's own events, so the two can be compared rather than one overwriting the other.
- Any company can verify a piece of evidence, using readings fetched from the company that holds
  them and an anchor read from the ledger with its own certificate.
- Temperature evidence may be submitted at any stage of a batch's life, because milk has to stay
  cold from the farm's tank to the retailer's fridge, not only in the truck. A recalled batch is
  the exception, since it has been withdrawn.
- Unsafe evidence must mark the batch as `COLD_CHAIN_BREACH`.
- Clearing a breach returns the batch to the stage it was at when the breach was recorded.
- A breached or recalled batch cannot be delivered.
- The regulator can recall a batch.
- Every event must include the Fabric transaction ID, transaction timestamp and invoking
  stakeholder identity.
- Certificates, private keys and generated blockchain artifacts are never committed.

## Known limitations

This is a proof of concept. The things it does not do, and what it would take to do them:

- **Readings dropped from the end of a run are not detected.** Each reading is signed by the sensor
  and numbered, so an altered value breaks its signature and a reading removed from the middle
  leaves a visible gap. Truncating the tail leaves what remains contiguous and correctly signed.
  Closing it needs the sensor to sign a session-close record, or the regulator to notice that an
  expected submission never arrived.
- **The sensor keys are generated by this repository**, like every certificate on the network. That
  relocates trust to whoever holds the sensor key rather than removing it, which is what security
  always does: the oracle genuinely cannot forge a signature for a key it does not hold, whoever
  made the key. In a real deployment the key is generated inside the device and never leaves it.
- **The readings endpoint does not authenticate its caller.** Anyone who can reach the oracle's
  backend and knows an evidence ID gets the readings. The ID contains part of the hash so it is
  not enumerable, but that is obscurity rather than access control. The verification is sound
  regardless: the checker recomputes the fingerprint and compares it against the ledger, so a
  dishonest oracle can refuse to answer but cannot make altered readings verify.
- **The chain never refuses a lying oracle, it only makes the lie provable.** To check a signature
  a peer would need the readings, and anything sent to a peer is kept in a block forever, which is
  what off-chain storage exists to avoid. Fabric's answer is private data collections. They are
  built for confidential business data rather than bulk telemetry, and adopting them would move the
  readings into the ledger and leave the off-chain store doing nothing. So this is **detection, not
  prevention**: the regulator finds out within seconds, and any company can prove it afterwards.
- **No user authentication and no TLS on the backends.** Each backend's Fabric identity is fixed
  at startup, so a caller cannot impersonate a company, but nor is there anything deciding which
  humans may drive a given company's backend. A real deployment authenticates the operator and
  serves over HTTPS.
- **Both databases share one PostgreSQL container.** Separate databases with separate logins,
  which is enough to show the isolation, but one superuser can still reach both. Real separation
  means separate instances under separate control.
- **The demo control panel is a harness, not a product.** In reality each company would run its
  own application. One page driving six backends is a convenience for showing the system in five
  minutes.
